This thesis asked whether the media that learners already consume habitually --- short-form video and hypercasual games --- can be turned into an effective, personalised vocabulary-learning environment (Chapter~\ref{chapter:intro}). It answered by designing and building \textit{Glosy}, a cross-platform mobile application in which a single word-level learner model, holding an FSRS memory state for every word a learner tracks, drives both the selection of reels and the words of every game round. This chapter states what the work contributes, what it does not yet establish, and where it should go next.

\section{Contributions}
\label{sec:contributions}
    The contributions of this thesis are the following.

    \begin{enumerate}
        \item \textbf{A word-level learner model shared by content and play.} Every word a learner tracks carries a Free Spaced Repetition Scheduler (FSRS) state, updated on the device after each graded review, so the application works offline and synchronises when a connection returns (Sections~\ref{subsec:offline_impl} and~\ref{subsec:sync_impl}). Reel transcripts are tokenised and linked to the same word identifiers, so that a word tapped in a subtitle, a word due for review, and a word in a game round are one and the same record.
        \item \textbf{A machine-checkable operationalisation of comprehensible input.} The recommender keeps a reel only if the learner already knows at least 98\% of its words, turning Krashen's qualitative $i+1$ boundary into a computable filter grounded in the lexical-coverage result of Hu and Nation (Section~\ref{subsec:comprehensible_input}). It is the first stage of a three-stage cascade in Burke's sense: a hard comprehensibility filter, then a spaced-repetition stage that ranks reels containing due words first, then a content-based stage that breaks ties by TF-IDF cosine similarity to the learner's engagement history. Each stage degrades to a constant when it has no signal, so the cascade never fails for a new learner (Section~\ref{subsec:cascade_design}).
        \item \textbf{Games whose rounds are spaced-repetition reviews.} A multilingual Wordle-style game, with per-language alphabets and a right-to-left board and keyboard for Farsi, and Word of Wonders, a crossword generated on the device from the learner's own vocabulary under a letter budget, both draw their words from the tracked vocabulary and preferentially from the words FSRS marks as due (Section~\ref{sec:games_design}). The win condition of a round and the FSRS review coincide, rather than the game merely being themed around vocabulary.
        \item \textbf{A content pipeline that keeps the request path light.} Learners can create reels in the application, with timestamped subtitles and translations, and edit them after publishing. Tokenisation and lemmatisation, which need spaCy, Stanza, or Hazm, run in an offline backfill rather than in the reels service, so that the service fits a 512~MB host and depends only on scikit-learn's lightweight vectorisers at request time (Sections~\ref{subsec:reels_stack} and~\ref{subsec:reel_creation_impl}).
        \item \textbf{A working, multilingual prototype.} \textit{Glosy} is a deployed three-service system (React Native frontend, Node.js backend, FastAPI reels service) over one PostgreSQL schema, released as an Android application. It supports English, Greek, and Farsi, including a right-to-left script, in any native/learning combination, with a dictionary of roughly 41{,}000 words tagged by CEFR level.
        \item \textbf{A component-level evaluation that locates the real bottlenecks.} Since no study with learners exists, Chapter~\ref{chapter:evaluation_results} evaluated the algorithms directly. Across 4{,}200 trials the crossword generator never failed, and the richness of its boards was bounded by the letter budget rather than the word cap. The recommender's Stage~1 and Stage~2 behaved as designed, but what limited them was the size and tokenisation of the catalogue, not the ranking logic.
    \end{enumerate}

    \subsection{Answers to the Research Questions}
    \label{subsec:rq_answers}
    \textbf{RQ1} asked how a mobile application can integrate authentic short-form video with a word-level vocabulary model. The answer given here is architectural: reels carry timestamped, tokenised, translated transcripts whose tokens are the rows of the learner's own vocabulary table, so that watching a reel can both read from and write to the learner model. \textbf{RQ2} asked how a recommendation engine should select reels that are both comprehensible and lexically challenging. The answer is the cascade above, whose ordering --- comprehensibility first, due words second, similarity last --- is justified by the memory and input theory of Chapter~\ref{chapter:related_work}, and whose behaviour on a real, if small, catalogue was tested in Chapter~\ref{chapter:evaluation_results}. \textbf{RQ3} asked to what extent hypercasual mechanics, parameterised by the learner's vocabulary state, can serve as effective spaced-repetition exercises. This thesis shows that they can be built and that the generator is robust at every tested vocabulary size, but the word \emph{effective} cannot be answered without learners: whether these rounds improve retention is precisely the question the missing user study would address (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
    The work presented in this thesis has several limitations that must be acknowledged honestly.

    \subsection{Evidence and Content}
    \begin{sloppypar}
    \textbf{No empirical user study.} The pedagogical effectiveness of the system has not been evaluated in a controlled experiment. Chapter~\ref{chapter:evaluation_results} shows that the components behave as designed, not that learners gain or retain vocabulary, or that they stay engaged. Design parameters taken from the literature --- the 98\% coverage threshold and the default FSRS parameters --- have likewise not been validated on \textit{Glosy}'s own learners.

    \textbf{Limited content library.} The current content library is limited in scope and diversity. The catalogue evaluated in Chapter~\ref{chapter:evaluation_results} holds 56 Farsi reels, added by a maintainer to demonstrate the filter rather than curated as a learning library. \textit{Glosy} faces the cold-start problem of any new application \cite{adomavicius2005toward}: with no user base yet, no learners have uploaded reels, so the library stays small, and the recommender in turn has few reels to choose among and no interaction history to personalise from. The dictionary is larger, but it was assembled from published word and frequency lists with the help of a large language model, and its entries and translations have not been independently audited in this work.
    \end{sloppypar}

    \subsection{Language Processing}
    \begin{sloppypar}
    \textbf{Tokenisation is incomplete for Farsi.} Some languages differ in register between speech and writing, and attach affixes to words to express what other languages express with separate words. Farsi does both: its colloquial forms differ systematically from the written standard, and a suffix can turn \textit{m\={a}dar} (mother) into \textit{m\={a}daram} (my mother). English and Greek raise this problem to a much smaller degree. The Farsi tokeniser used here, Hazm, does not recognise every word form of this kind, even when the corresponding word is in the \texttt{words} table, despite the informal-text normalisation and possessive-suffix expansion added to it (Section~\ref{subsec:reel_creation_impl}). A token that is not recognised is not stored, and this has two consequences. First, the word appears in the subtitles as plain text: it cannot be tapped, translated, or added to the learner's vocabulary. Second, it is left out of the reel's word set, so Stage~1's coverage is measured over the words the pipeline recognised, not over every word spoken in the reel, and the displayed comprehensibility percentage can overstate how much of the reel the learner understands. How often this occurs has not been quantified here.
    \end{sloppypar}

    % >>> SUGGESTED (confirm): homograph linking
    \textbf{No word-sense disambiguation.} When a lemma matches more than one dictionary entry, the pipeline links the entry with the lowest identifier, without regard to part of speech or meaning, so a token can be linked to the wrong homograph.

    \textbf{Offline annotation of new reels.} A newly created reel has no token data until the backfill script has run, and until then it is invisible to the personalised feed (Section~\ref{subsec:personalisation_status}). Publishing is therefore not yet self-service.

    \subsection{Recommendation and Progression}
    % >>> SUGGESTED (confirm): Stage 3 signals that are never recorded
    \textbf{Stage 3 has fewer signals than designed.} The profile vector weights views, likes, saves, shares, and comments (Section~\ref{subsec:stage3_design}), but only views, likes, and saves are currently recorded. The share button opens the operating system's share sheet without recording anything, and the comment sheet is an interface mock-up without a backend. Together with the absence of any interaction history (Section~\ref{subsec:recommender_cold_start}), this means Stage~3 currently contributes nothing to the ranking of any learner.

    % >>> SUGGESTED (confirm): self-declared level as ground truth
    \textbf{Vocabulary is seeded from a self-declared level.} Registration assumes that a learner who declares B1 already knows every A1 and A2 word (Section~\ref{subsec:registration_impl}). A learner who overestimates their level starts with an inflated known-word set, so Stage~1 lets through reels that are too hard for them; the seeded FSRS states are also assumptions, not observations. Stage~1 also counts any tracked word as known, whatever its FSRS retrievability, which is deliberate (Section~\ref{subsec:stage1_design}) but means a word that has since been forgotten still counts towards coverage.

    \textbf{Incomplete progression system.} Only coins have a complete reward loop, and their amounts differ between the games (Section~\ref{subsec:currencies_design}). Energy and experience are stored and displayed, but nothing spends or increments them. The Wordle-style game grades a win as a fixed \texttt{Good} rating and records nothing on a loss (Section~\ref{subsec:game_review_design}), a coarser signal than the self-graded prompt of Word of Wonders.

    \textbf{Scaling of the current implementation.} The recommender scores the whole candidate pool in memory on every request, and the offline synchronisation endpoint updates vocabulary one word at a time (Sections~\ref{subsec:personalisation_status} and~\ref{subsec:sync_impl}). The crossword generator runs on the JavaScript thread, and its cost grows superlinearly with vocabulary size; it was measured under Node.js on a laptop, not on a phone (Section~\ref{subsubsec:wow_eval_gen_time}). None of these matters at the present scale, but each would at a larger one.

    \subsection{Onboarding and Social Scaffolding}
    \textbf{Steep onboarding barriers.} Like LingQ, \textit{Glosy} builds on authentic material, and such an interface poses steep onboarding barriers for novice learners \cite{karasimos2022battle}. The evaluation shows this directly: at the 98\% threshold only 4\% of the tokenised reels pass for a learner who declares no prior knowledge and 12\% at A1, and a new learner's Word of Wonders board holds only one or two words (Sections~\ref{subsec:stage1_eval} and~\ref{subsubsec:wow_eval_words_placed}). A looser threshold does not close this gap; easier content does.

    \textbf{Lack of social scaffolding.} The system lacks mechanisms for social scaffolding through peer or native-speaker interaction. Learners can like, save, and share a reel, but they cannot yet ask a question about it or receive a correction, so the sociocultural side of the zone of proximal development (Section~\ref{subsec:sociocultural_theory}) is approximated only by the lexical threshold and not by human support. The planned remedy is the reel's comment section: the comment sheet is already part of the interface, but it is not yet connected to a backend. Once it is, a learner who meets an idiom or a cultural reference in a reel can ask about it directly beneath that reel, where the creator or another learner can answer, which is the content-attached scaffolding that Section~\ref{subsec:platform_limitations} found missing from existing platforms.

    % >>> SUGGESTED (confirm): scope of language support
    \textbf{Three languages, each hand-configured.} Only English, Greek, and Farsi are supported, and each language has its own hand-written configuration: a letter pattern and keyboard for Wordle, a lemmatiser for tokenisation, and its own entry in the language enumeration of the API and in the onboarding options. The Wordle-style game also fixes the secret word at five letters.

\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:future_work}
    The following directions are identified as high-value:
    \begin{sloppypar}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Empirical evaluation:} A randomised controlled study measuring vocabulary gain, retention at one week and one month, and learner engagement metrics, comparing \textit{Glosy} against a no-game control condition and against Duolingo. It would also test whether the 98\% threshold is the right value for a video-based learner and whether the game rounds improve on passive review.
        \item \textbf{Automated content annotation pipeline:} A pipeline that ingests a raw video URL, transcribes speech using a large language model, aligns the transcript to video timestamps, tokenises and POS-tags the output, maps tokens to the word database, and produces a fully annotated reel record---reducing the per-reel curation cost from hours to minutes, and removing the manual backfill step that currently keeps new reels out of the personalised feed.
        \item \textbf{Manual tokenisation while creating a reel:} Letting the creator correct the tokenisation of a subtitle line inside the reel-creation flow, by marking which dictionary word each unrecognised word belongs to. This would recover the words that Farsi's colloquial forms and attached suffixes cause the tokeniser to miss (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}), make them tappable, and keep them in the coverage calculation; the creator's corrections could also serve as training data for a better tokeniser.
        \item \textbf{Coverage over all running words:} Storing unrecognised tokens as such, instead of dropping them, so that Stage~1 can measure coverage over every word of a transcript and report a comprehensibility percentage that does not depend on the tokeniser's recall.
        \item \textbf{Cold-start mitigation via onboarding preferences:} Wiring the topic preferences and age already collected by onboarding's \texttt{PersonalizationSlide.tsx} (Section~\ref{subsec:recommender_cold_start}) into Stage~3 as a cold-start substitute for the interaction-based profile vector, replacing its current fixed, hand-picked eight-topic taxonomy with automated topic generation derived from reel content once the library is large enough to support it.
        \item \textbf{Age-conditioned recommendation:} Using the \texttt{age} column, already collected at onboarding but currently unread by the recommendation engine, as a further ranking or filtering signal once a concrete policy for it is designed.
        \item \textbf{Comment section and collaborative learning features:} Connecting the reel comment section to a backend, so that learners can ask questions and receive answers from the creator or from other learners beneath the reel they concern, which is the main remedy for the missing social scaffolding (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}). Beyond it, shared vocabulary challenges, leaderboards scoped to friend groups, and discussion attached to individual words would extend the social dimension of Vygotsky's theory. Persisting comments and shares would also give Stage~3 the two signals it is currently designed for but never receives.
        \item \textbf{Multi-language expansion:} The schema is language-agnostic (language is a foreign key throughout), so a new language needs mainly content ingestion. It also needs a small amount of per-language configuration --- a lemmatiser, a Wordle letter pattern and keyboard layout, and an entry in the language enumeration --- which could be moved into the database so that adding a language stops requiring a release.
        % use Cache like Redis
        \item \textbf{Server-side game generation and recommendation scoring:} Generating Word of Wonders boards on the server and pushing them to the device while it is online, so that generation cost no longer depends on the phone (Section~\ref{subsubsec:wow_eval_gen_time}), and moving Stage~3's TF-IDF computation into SQL or a cache once the catalogue outgrows in-memory scoring.
        % >>> SUGGESTED (confirm): complete the energy/experience loop
        \item \textbf{A complete progression loop:} Unifying the coin schedule across the games and either attaching a gameplay loop to energy and experience, as the schema and configuration already anticipate (Section~\ref{subsec:currencies_design}), or removing them.
        % >>> SUGGESTED (confirm): validating the seeded vocabulary
        \item \textbf{Placement instead of self-declaration:} Replacing the self-declared proficiency level with a short adaptive placement test, so that the seeded vocabulary and its FSRS states start from observed rather than assumed knowledge.
    \end{itemize}
    \end{sloppypar}

\section{Concluding Remarks}
\label{sec:concluding_remarks}
    \textit{Glosy} shows that a single word-level memory model can connect the two places where learners already spend their attention: the videos they watch choose which words are due, and the games they play review those same words. Whether this makes learners retain more than they would with existing applications is not yet known, and the limitations above show that a larger, better-annotated content library is the first thing to build before that can be tested. The thesis's claim is therefore a modest one: the design is coherent, implemented, and robust at the component level, and it is ready to be tested with learners.
